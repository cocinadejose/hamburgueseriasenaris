Galician octopus: my home recipe without a copper pot

Published on 13 November 2026 · By Susana

Let me be straight with you from the start: what I am about to share is not pulpo a feira as you would eat it at a real Galician fair (a feira is a village festival or market day). That version is sacred, cooked by women called pulpeiras who have spent a lifetime boiling octopus in copper pots. For the real thing, head to one of the Galician food festivals, a local fiesta in Santiago or the surrounding villages, or a proper pulpeira. What follows is simply my way of cooking Galician-style octopus at home, with ordinary pans and no fuss.

Why octopus has become a luxury in Galicia

My husband Alejandro is a taxi driver here in Santiago. His brother sells octopus wholesale and even exports octopus from the Galician rías (the tidal inlets along our coast) to other countries. With the gift that man has for selling, it is no surprise that octopus in Galicia has turned into a food for special occasions or for people with deep pockets. These days even the village pulpeiras, once the most affordable places you could imagine, are becoming too expensive for everyday eating.

I say it as a joke, but I am only half laughing: before long we will see a quarter portion of octopus on menus as if it were normal — two slices of octopus, half a potato and a glass of wine — because that is all many people will be able to afford. And that is a shame, because octopus used to belong to everyone here.

Why do we not serve it every day at Señarís?

I have cooked it for special occasions and served it as a pincho, but honestly the price goes through the roof and it stops making sense. Serving a single slice feels stingy, and I would rather give you something that actually leaves you happy. Our zorza, a good Spanish tortilla or one of our burgers are far more honest for a normal day.

My Galician-style octopus recipe

Again, this is Galician-style octopus, not pulpo a feira. The word feira says it all: to eat it a feira, you have to go to the feira. My version is what I do in a normal kitchen, with normal pans.

Ingredients (serves 4)

  • 1 fresh or frozen octopus, about 1.5–2 kg, cleaned
  • Water to cover (no salt)
  • 1 whole head of garlic
  • 1 onion
  • Cooked potatoes or Galician cachelos, to serve
  • Extra-virgin olive oil
  • Sweet paprika, and a little hot paprika if you like
  • Coarse sea salt flakes, to sprinkle

Step by step

1. Freeze it first if it is fresh. If your octopus is fresh and you do not want to beat it, put it in the freezer overnight. The cold breaks the fibres, so you do not have to spend half the morning hitting the poor creature. It already has enough to do becoming our dinner.

2. Thaw it. If it was frozen, thaw it in the fridge or under cold water. That also helps the fibres open up and the octopus becomes tender.

3. Prepare the water. Fill a normal pan with water. I do not own a copper pot, so I use one of our everyday kitchen pans. Important: do not add salt to the cooking water. Salt toughens the octopus and can make the skin slip off. Just add one whole head of garlic and one onion, washed but not peeled or chopped. That gives the broth a lovely depth.

4. Scare the octopus. When the water is boiling hard, hold the octopus by the head and dip it in and out of the water three times. This fixes the skin to the flesh so it does not peel during cooking, and it also makes the tips of the tentacles curl into that curled shape everyone recognises on a plate of Galician octopus.

5. Cook it. Leave the octopus in the water and keep it at a gentle boil. People say about 20 minutes per kilo, but I never really time it. My test is to push a skewer into the thickest part of a tentacle; it should go in easily. Always test the thick part, because the thin part can trick you into thinking it is done when the thick part is still raw.

6. Rest. When it is ready, lift it out and let it rest for a few minutes. Do not leave it sitting in the hot water or it will keep cooking.

7. Slice and plate. Cut the tentacles into slices about a finger thick. Lay them over the potatoes, drizzle with extra-virgin olive oil, sprinkle with sweet paprika (and a pinch of hot paprika if you want) and finish with a little coarse salt.

The potato trick

I cook the potatoes separately. The idea is that the potato flavour should not compete with the octopus. It should be a support act, not the main star. If you like, you can cook them in a little of the octopus water, but do not overdo it or they will turn to mash.

Do not throw away the octopus water

This is crucial: the water you cooked the octopus in is liquid gold. It carries the flavour of the garlic, onion and the octopus itself. That broth will take a rice stew to another level. Or a fish soup. Or even potatoes boiled in it. Throwing it away would be a crime.

Where to eat real pulpo a feira

If you want the real thing, cooked by a pulpeira with decades of experience, your best plan is to visit the Galician gastronomic festivals and the fiestas of Santiago and the villages. There you will find octopus boiled in copper pots, served on wooden boards, with potatoes and local wine. That experience cannot — and should not — be copied at home.
