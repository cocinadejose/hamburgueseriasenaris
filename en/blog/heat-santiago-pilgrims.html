📢 2026: a year that's going to hit hard

Around San Juan week, June 23 and 24 and the days around them, it was crazy hot in Santiago. I was behind the bar with the fans on full blast and still sweating buckets. And that was just June. I don't even want to think about what July and August will bring.

Weather forecasts say 2026 is going to be an exceptionally hot year across the Iberian Peninsula. Longer heatwaves, higher temperatures, and that Galician humidity that turns 30 degrees into a stifling 35. If you're planning to do the Camino this summer, you need to be prepared. That's not an exaggeration. It's just how it is.

I've been behind a bar in Santiago for over 25 years, watching pilgrims arrive. I've seen it all. People who show up fresh as a daisy, and people who stumble in with sunken faces, dry lips and a dazed look. The difference between them isn't luck — it's preparation. And this year, with the heat that's coming, preparation is what will make the difference between enjoying the Camino and having a rough time. Seriously.

When you come is your call. Not every year is the same, and every body handles things differently. After many summers here, my observation is that the trend is getting hotter. If you handle high temperatures well, go for it. If not, consider May, June or September. You decide. But whatever you decide, this article will help you come prepared.

With that said, let's get to the point. First I'll tell you how to survive the Camino, then what to do when you reach Santiago and the city is an oven.

🥾 Before you set out: the heat on the Camino is no joke

Let me be clear: doing the Camino de Santiago in the middle of summer without accounting for the heat is reckless. Your body has limits, and the heat tests them every day.

Lots of people come from the Netherlands, Germany, the UK, or from the Castilian plateau... and they think Galicia is green, cool and rainy. And it is — in winter. In summer, inland Galicia is a trap: humid heat, no breeze, and stretches where there isn't a patch of shade for miles.

The routes: not all are equal

If you're coming on the French Way from Sarria (the most popular route, about 114 km in 5-6 stages), keep this in mind: it's rural country with small villages. The stage from Palas de Rei to Arzúa is 29 km, the longest of the whole stretch, and has sub-sections with no shade or bar for 10-15 km. If you don't carry enough water, you're done for. Literally. The French Way is packed in summer: full hostels, queues at bars, and the sun beating down on open fields without mercy.

The Portuguese Way from Tui (about 119 km, 5-6 stages) is a smarter option if you're coming in the middle of July or August. The terrain is flatter, there are more shaded forest sections (eucalyptus, oaks, pines), and pilgrim traffic is lighter than on the French Way. The villages have good services and aren't as crowded. The first stage, Tui to O Porriño, is just 16 km. The highlight stage is Redondela to Pontevedra: beautiful forests, spontaneous fruit and drink stalls, and the medieval bridge of Pontesampaio. That said, watch out for some asphalt sections near the industrial areas of O Porriño.

If it's your first Camino and you're coming in summer, my recommendation is clear: do the Portuguese Way from Tui. You'll suffer less and enjoy it more. The French Way from Sarria is beautiful but in July-August it turns into a procession under the sun.

The Northern Way has the Cantabrian breeze working in its favor, but the asphalt sections under the midday sun are brutal. And the Primitive Way, with its steep climbs and widely spaced villages, under extreme heat is for experienced people in very good shape. I wouldn't go there in August unless you know what you're getting into.

Weekends: watch out for supermarkets

This is important and almost nobody mentions it: in small Galician villages, many supermarkets and shops close on Saturday afternoons and all day Sunday. If your stage falls on a weekend, don't count on being able to buy water or food mid-way. Stock up the day before or set out well-loaded in the morning. I've seen more than one pilgrim walk through three villages on a Sunday looking for a bottle of water. Not a good plan.

Before you set out each morning: check what day of the week it is, look up the stage on your phone (apps like Buen Camino or Gronze), and identify the spots with services. If you see a long stretch with nothing, take double the water.

🎒 What to pack in your backpack

I've seen people arrive with blisters the size of two-euro coins (about 25mm across) and neck burns that look second-degree. No joke. The Galician sun hits hard in summer, and if you're coming from a cloudy climate, you don't expect it.

Item Why Don't bring
Metal flask (1.5-2L) Plastic heats up water in 10 minutes in the sun. Metal keeps it cool. Carry two if the stage is long. Thin plastic bottles.
Wide-brimmed hat Protects neck, ears and face. The sun is directly overhead in summer. Baseball cap (doesn't cover the neck).
SPF 50+ sunscreen Reapply every 2 hours, even if cloudy. UV radiation goes through clouds. Low SPF or relying on "I'm already tanned".
Technical t-shirts (2-3) Quick-drying. Cotton gets soaked, weighs you down and chafes. Sounds like a Decathlon ad but it's true. Cotton for walking.
Spare socks Changing them mid-stage saves your feet. Moisture is the mother of blisters. One pair for the whole day.
Sunglasses The sun bounces off asphalt and white gravel. Eye fatigue = mental fatigue. Going without on exposed stretches.
Basic first-aid kit Plasters, antiseptic, sterile needle, Compeed for blisters. Pharmacies aren't common in rural areas. Thinking "I'll buy stuff if I need it".
Weight matters more than ever: in the heat, every extra kilo in your backpack is extra suffering. If you're unsure whether to bring something, don't. The 10% of body weight rule is even more important in summer.

🚶 On the trail: timing, water and food

⏰ Timing is everything

In summer, getting up early isn't an option — it's survival. The ideal schedule is to leave before 6:00 AM. Yes, you read that right. It's still dark at that hour, but within an hour it starts to get light and the air is cool. Walking between 6:00 and 11:00 AM is the difference between arriving in good shape or arriving wrecked.

Time What to do Why
5:30 - 6:00 Leave the hostel Cool, no sun. The first kilometers go by themselves.
6:00 - 11:00 Walk most of the stage Good hours. Make progress.
11:00 - 12:00 Finish the stage if possible The sun starts to hit hard. Find shade.
12:00 - 17:00 Rest. Bar, siesta, anything but walking. Highest risk hours. Don't walk in the sun.
17:00 - 20:00 If you have stage left, now or never. Still hot but more bearable.
If the hostel doesn't open until 1:00 PM and you arrive early, find a shady bar or park. Lying on a bench in the shade for an hour isn't wasting time — it's an investment in your body.

💧 Hydration: don't wait until you're thirsty

By the time you're thirsty, you're already dehydrated. That's how it works. Take small sips every 15-20 minutes even if you're not thirsty. If you wait until your mouth is dry, you're already behind.

  • At least 2 liters of water per stage. If the stage is long or it's very hot, 3 liters. Don't skimp on weight when it comes to water — it's the only load worth carrying.
  • Make use of village fountains. In Galicia, tap water is drinkable and good. Most public fountains are safe to drink from, but always check: if you see a sign saying "water not suitable for human consumption", don't drink from it and find the next one. Refill whenever you can.
  • Avoid alcohol while walking. A cold beer when you arrive is a reward, but during the stage, stick to water. Alcohol dehydrates you and in the heat it's shooting yourself in the foot.
  • Mineral salts. If you sweat a lot, water alone isn't enough. Bring electrolyte tablets or isotonic drink powder. One sachet in your flask and you're good.

🚚 The trick many people don't know: have your backpack transported

There's a service that's a game-changer in summer: backpack transport stage by stage. Here's how it works: each morning you leave your backpack at the hostel reception before 8:00 AM, a van picks it up and drops it at your next accommodation before 2:30 PM. You walk with just a small daypack of 3-4 kg (water, food, documents and not much else).

The main companies are Correos (Paq Mochila) and Jacotrans. The price is around 5-7€ per stage and backpack. For the last 100 km from Sarria (5 stages), that's about 30-35€ total. Not a fortune. And the difference between walking with 10 kg or with 3 kg under a 35-degree sun is huge.

Lots of people think using this service is "cheating". It's not. It's doing the Camino in a way that's sustainable for your body. I've seen too many people give up on the Camino by day three because of back or knee injuries that would have been avoided by shedding 7 kilos. If the heat is already going to punish you, at least don't add unnecessary weight.

🏨 Hostels: not all are equal when it's hot

Public Xunta hostels usually don't have air conditioning. They're bunk beds in shared rooms, a fan if you're lucky, and in summer they turn into ovens at night. If you can, every 2 or 3 days treat yourself to a private hostel or guesthouse with air conditioning. They cost around 15-25€ and the difference in rest is huge. A pilgrim who sleeps well makes better decisions the next day. One who hasn't slept in three nights because of the heat is a danger to themselves.

In peak season (July-August), book private hostels 2-3 months in advance. Public ones can't be booked, so if you're relying on them, arrive before 1:00 PM or you'll be left without a bed.

🥗 Eating without weighing yourself down

Light meals. Skip the pilgrim menus with starter, main, dessert and wine at two in the afternoon. I've seen people walk out of restaurants looking like zombies. In summer your body digests worse, and feeding it a heavy stew or grilled meat at 2:00 PM with 35-degree heat is a death sentence. Fruit, sandwiches, salads, nuts. Eat less and more often.

🚕 Broken by the heat and can't go on? Susana's husband, Alejandro — like the Macedonian king but without the empire — is a taxi driver in Santiago. If you're on a stage near the city and can't continue, or if you arrive in Santiago and need a ride to the beach, the doctor, or wherever, call him directly at +34 685 13 63 43. He always has his phone handy.

⚠️ Heatstroke: warning signs

This isn't a filler section. Heatstroke kills. Every year there are pilgrims who need to be evacuated, and some who don't make it. Your body has limits, and with Galician humidity, that limit comes sooner than you think.

Warning signs — if you or someone else has these, STOP walking NOW:
  • Severe dizziness or loss of balance
  • Throbbing headache that won't go away with water
  • Nausea or vomiting
  • Cold, clammy skin, even though it's blazing hot
  • Mental confusion: not knowing where you are, talking nonsense
  • Very fast or very weak pulse
  • Suddenly stopping sweating (this is very serious)

What to do: call 112 immediately. It's free, 24 hours, and they handle English and other languages. While help arrives, lay the person in the shade, wet their neck, armpits and groin with cool (not ice-cold) water, and give them water in small sips if they're conscious. Don't leave them alone.

And if you're running out of water and far from anywhere: don't be shy. Go into a private house, a bar, a shop, wherever you see people. No one in Galicia will refuse a glass of water to a pilgrim. Even if you have no money, even if you're not going to buy anything. Don't risk it out of shyness. People here understand, and we help each other out.

Pharmacies on the Camino: in small villages, the pharmacy is usually on the main street and closes at midday (2:00 PM to 5:00 PM, like everything in Spain). If you need something, go before midday or after five. On Sundays most are closed. Look for the "farmacia de guardia" (duty pharmacy) sign in any pharmacy window — it tells you which one is open.

🏙️ Arriving in Santiago: the city burns too

You've made it. Congratulations. You've survived the stages, the blisters, the sun and the shadeless stretches. But Santiago in summer is another battle. The city sits in an inland valley, no sea nearby, and the old town's stone absorbs heat and releases it at night like a radiator. Don't relax just yet.

⏰ Survival schedules in the city

The same logic as the Camino applies here:

  • Before 11:00 AM: visit the Cathedral, pick up your Compostela, stroll. The city is cool and half-empty. The Cathedral at 7:00 AM is another dimension: no queues, no noise, no heat.
  • 11:00 AM to 2:00 PM: still doable, but look for shade. Parks, museums, terraces with awnings.
  • 2:00 PM to 5:00 PM: disappear. These are the worst hours. Find a museum with air conditioning, the swimming pool, or take a siesta. But don't walk around with your backpack at three in the afternoon. I've seen it too many times and it makes no sense.
  • After 7:30 PM: the city comes alive. The old town cools down, people come out, terraces fill up. This is the time to enjoy.

💧 Where to cool off in Santiago

🏊 Fontes do Sar Swimming Pools

A 20-minute walk from the center, in the Sar neighborhood. Olympic pool, kids' area and snack bar. Entry: 3-4€. Swim cap required. How to get there: from the Cathedral, go down Rúa das Casas Reais towards the Sar river and follow the park. The water is cold and worth every cent.

Cold water fountains in the old town:

  • Praza das Praterías: next to the Cathedral. Fresh, drinkable water. One of the best.
  • Praza do Obradoiro: in the gardens of the Hostal dos Reis Católicos.
  • Praza de Quintana: the lower fountain, the one at Quintana dos Mortos.
Watch out with fountains: make sure there's no sign saying "water not suitable for human consumption". In Galicia most are drinkable, but some ornamental or very old fountains might not be. If you see the sign, don't drink from it and find the next one.

Parks to lie down in the shade: Alameda (the classic, full of pilgrims), Belvís Park (quieter), Parque de la Música (near the train station).

Museums with air conditioning: if you can't take it anymore between 2:00 PM and 5:00 PM, museums are your salvation. Check out our guide to museums and cultural activities.

🏖️ If you need to escape to the beach

Sometimes the city gets to you. You've sweated more than you thought possible and you need to see the sea. I completely understand. If you can't take it anymore, here's our beach guide. Vilagarcía de Arousa is 20 minutes by train. The water is cold, the breeze blows, and Santiago feels like a distant memory.

Options: Noia beaches (Testal, Boa), Outes river pools (freshwater and trees), and of course Finisterre. Many pilgrims extend the Camino to Fisterra and Muxía. If you have a few extra days, do it. Seeing the ocean after the Camino is another reward entirely.

🗣️ Local insider tips

These are things you won't find in the guides, and they're what really save you:

  • The Cathedral's shadow at one in the afternoon is the best spot in Santiago. The Obradoiro façade casts a huge shadow. Sit on the steps on the left, next to the pilgrim office. There's a breeze and no one bothers you.
  • Tap water in Galicia is genuinely good. It comes from mountain springs. Don't waste money on plastic bottles. Refill your flask at any fountain.
  • In a hostel with no fan: wet a towel, wring it out, and put it over you to sleep. It's tacky but it works. And bring earplugs: in summer the noise in the old town doesn't stop until late.
  • Between two and five, take shelter. Don't walk the streets at those hours unless you want to end up wrecked. Go to a shopping center, a tapas bar with air conditioning, a museum... anything but staying exposed to the sun. Those of us who live here do the same.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Is it safe to do the Camino in summer with the heat expected in 2026?

Yes, but with preparation. Forecasts point to an exceptionally hot year. The key: walk at dawn (leave before 6:00 AM), avoid the sun between 12:00 and 5:00 PM, carry at least 2 liters of water per stage, and know the signs of heatstroke. Rural stretches, especially on the French Way from Sarria, have few water points.

Can I buy water and food during the stages?

It depends on the stage and the day of the week. There are 10-15 km stretches with no services at all, especially on the French Way from Sarria. Plus, in small villages many supermarkets close on Saturday afternoons and all day Sunday. Set out each morning with enough water and some food (fruit, nuts, energy bars).

What do I pack in my backpack for the heat?

Metal flask (1.5-2 liters), wide-brimmed hat, SPF 50+ sunscreen, sunglasses, quick-dry technical shirts, spare socks, and a first-aid kit with plasters and antiseptic. Avoid cotton for walking: it dries slowly and causes chafing. Every extra kilo multiplies in the heat.

What are the signs of heatstroke?

Dizziness, throbbing headache, nausea, cold clammy skin (even though it's hot), mental confusion, rapid or weak pulse, and in severe cases, stopping sweating. If it happens to you or another pilgrim, call 112. It's a real emergency. Don't wait.

What time should I walk in summer?

Leave before 6:00 AM and try to finish before 12:00 PM. Between 12:00 and 5:00 PM you shouldn't be walking. After 5:00 PM you can continue if you have stage left, but carefully. Getting up early isn't optional.

I'm in Santiago. Where do I cool off?

Fontes do Sar swimming pools (3-4€, swim cap required), cold water fountains at Praterías, Obradoiro and Quintana, shady parks (Alameda, Belvís, Parque de la Música), and museums with air conditioning. If you want the beach, Vilagarcía is 20 minutes by train.
