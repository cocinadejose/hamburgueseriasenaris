Santiago Cake: The Traditional Galician Recipe with Protected Geographical Indication

Published on January 31st, 2026 | Recipe prepared by Susana

Hello! I'm Susana, and today I want to share with you my version of Santiago Cake, the most emblematic dessert from Galicia and one of the most recognized in Spain. With its characteristic apostle's cross in powdered sugar, this flourless almond cake is a symbol of Galician pastry tradition dating back to the 16th century. Protected with a Protected Geographical Indication (PGI) since 2006, its recipe has remained nearly unchanged for over 400 years. While I follow the traditional base, I'll also share my personal touches that make it special.

📜 History of Santiago Cake

The first documented reference to this cake dates from 1577, although its origin is probably even earlier. It's found in the Codex Calixtinus, a 12th-century manuscript preserved in Santiago de Compostela Cathedral, which mentions almond-based recipes made by nuns in compostelan convents.

For centuries, this cake was made mainly in convents and traditional pastry shops in Santiago. The recipe passed from generation to generation, remaining practically unchanged: almonds, sugar, eggs, and the aromas of Galician citrus.

💡 Historical curiosity: The decoration with the Cross of Santiago didn't appear until the late 19th or early 20th century. Before that, the cakes were served without decoration or with a light dusting of sugar over the entire surface. The cross became popular as a distinctive element to differentiate it from other almond desserts.

In 2006, the European Union granted Santiago Cake Protected Geographical Indication (PGI) status, recognizing its historical link with Galicia and establishing requirements for its authentic preparation. Only cakes made in Galicia following the traditional recipe can bear the PGI seal.

🥜 The Ingredients: Quality Over Quantity

One of the wonders of Santiago Cake is its simplicity. With only 5 basic ingredients, you achieve an extraordinary result. The key lies in the quality of each component:

📝 Ingredients (24-26 cm / 9-10 inch pan)

  • ✓ 250 grams ground almonds (blanched, also called white almonds)
  • ✓ 250 grams sugar (traditional white refined sugar, or brown sugar for my version)
  • ✓ 5 large eggs (at room temperature)
  • ✓ Zest of 1 lemon (only the yellow part, no white pith)
  • ✓ Zest of 1/2 orange (traditional) or a splash of rum (my version)
  • ✓ Freshly grated cinnamon stick (my preference) or 1 teaspoon ground cinnamon (optional)
  • ✓ Butter for greasing the pan
  • ✓ Powdered sugar for decoration

Notes on the ingredients:

The almonds: It's essential to use blanched ground almonds (white almonds). The skin would impart a more bitter flavor and a less fine texture. For PGI certification, almonds must represent a minimum of 33% of the total weight. In our recipe, we use almost 40%, which gives a juicier cake.

The eggs: They must be at room temperature. Take them out of the refrigerator at least 30 minutes before. Cold eggs don't emulsify well with sugar and the cake can turn out denser.

The sugar (my preference): The traditional recipe uses white refined sugar, and the 1:1 ratio (equal amounts of almonds and sugar) is correct. However, I like to use brown sugar: it gives a rustic, caramelized touch that goes very well with the almonds. If you want to go traditional, use white refined sugar; if you want to try my version, use brown sugar.

The citrus (my personal touch): Lemon zest is essential. It adds freshness and enhances the almond flavor. As for the orange in the traditional recipe, I personally omit it and instead add a splash of rum (the one we have left over here at the restaurant). It gives a fruity flavor that goes very well with the cake. If you prefer to follow the traditional recipe, use orange zest. Important: if you use citrus, grate only the colored part, never the white part which is bitter.

The cinnamon (important): This is the most debated ingredient. The PGI recipe allows it to be made with or without cinnamon. In Santiago, traditional pastry shops usually include it, but many families omit it. I don't use ground cinnamon: I like to add a little grated cinnamon stick, which not only adds flavor but also brings a slight spiciness to the palate that makes a difference from ground cinnamon. If you use cinnamon, try this method, you'll see what a difference it makes.

Step-by-Step Preparation

1 Prepare the oven and pan

Preheat the oven to 175°C (350°F) with top and bottom heat, without fan. Generously grease a round springform pan, 24-26 cm (9-10 inches) in diameter, with butter. Don't use parchment paper on the base, butter is sufficient and gives better results.

Professional tip: Spread the butter with your fingers or paper towel, making sure to cover the corners of the pan well. This will facilitate unmolding.

2 Beat the eggs with sugar

In a large bowl, beat the 5 whole eggs with the sugar for 3-4 minutes with an electric mixer on medium-high speed. The mixture should become foamy and whitish, increasing considerably in volume. This step is crucial: the air we incorporate now will give the cake its fluffiness.

Common mistake: Not beating enough. The mixture should triple in volume and fall in ribbons when you lift the whisk. If you don't beat enough, the cake will be dense.

3 Incorporate the ground almonds

Add the 250 grams of ground almonds to the egg mixture. Here we change technique: instead of beating, stir with gentle folding motions using a silicone spatula or wooden spoon. Do it from bottom to top, as if "folding" the almonds with the egg air.

Important technique: Don't use an electric mixer at this stage. The goal is not to lose the air we've incorporated. Mix only until you see no lumps of almond, no more.

4 Add the aromatics

Incorporate the lemon zest. Here you have two options:

  • Traditional version: Add zest of 1/2 orange and 1 teaspoon ground cinnamon
  • My personal version: Instead of orange, I add a splash of rum (2-3 tablespoons), and instead of ground cinnamon, I use grated cinnamon stick (about 3-4 cm of stick grated). The rum gives a fruity touch and the cinnamon stick provides a slight spiciness that makes the difference.

Continue mixing with the same folding technique until everything is well integrated. The batter should be homogeneous but light, with a creamy consistency and an incredible aroma.

5 Pour into the pan

Pour all the batter into the previously greased pan. Smooth the surface with a spatula, but don't obsess: the oven will take care of leveling. Tap the pan gently against the counter 2-3 times to eliminate any large air bubbles.

6 Bake

Place the pan in the oven, in the center, and bake for 30-35 minutes. The exact time depends on your oven: the cake is ready when the surface is golden and slightly cracked in the center, and when inserting a toothpick in the middle, it comes out clean or with some moist crumbs (not liquid).

Caution: Don't open the oven during the first 25 minutes. The sudden temperature change can cause the cake to sink. After 25 minutes, you can check with the toothpick test.

The cake will rise during baking and then drop slightly as it cools. This is normal and part of the process.

7 Cool

Remove from the oven and let cool completely in the pan for at least 30-45 minutes. The cake is very fragile when hot and breaks easily. As it cools, it sets and acquires its characteristic texture: moist inside, with a slightly crunchy crust.

Once cool, run a knife around the edge of the pan to release and unmold carefully. The cake should unmold easily thanks to the butter.

8 Decorate with the Cross of Santiago

This is the most iconic step. Place a template with the shape of the Cross of Santiago in the center of the cake. Generously sprinkle powdered sugar over the entire surface using a fine strainer or sieve.

Carefully remove the template by lifting it vertically. The silhouette of the cross will remain in the natural color of the cake, contrasting with the white powdered sugar.

Professional trick: If you don't have a template, you can make the cross with two strips of paper in a + shape and achieve a similar result. You can also simply sprinkle powdered sugar over the entire cake, although you'll lose the traditional decorative element.

Serving and Storage

When to serve: Although it's delicious freshly made, Santiago Cake improves the next day. The flavors fully integrate and the texture sets. It's perfect as a dessert after a meal, or to accompany coffee or tea in the afternoon.

Traditional accompaniments:

  • Plain, as is (the most authentic way)
  • With a scoop of vanilla ice cream
  • With whipped cream lightly sweetened
  • With a glass of sweet Albariño or Ribeiro wine
  • With traditional Galician coffee

Traditional Galician tip: Years ago it was common to put a little sweet wine under the cake. You pour it on the plate and the cake absorbs that sweet wine. If you can't find the traditional Sansón brand, you can use any type of sweet wine, whiskey, or rum, whatever you prefer. Never pour it on top; pour a little on the plate and place the slice of cake on top, letting the cake absorb for a couple of minutes. This technique intensifies the flavor and adds a special dimension to the tasting experience.

Storage: The cake keeps perfectly for 5-7 days at room temperature in an airtight container or covered with plastic wrap. It's not necessary to refrigerate it. In fact, cold can harden it. Its high sugar and almond content acts as a natural preservative.

Freezing: It can be frozen for up to 3 months. Wrap it well in plastic wrap and aluminum foil. Thaw at room temperature for 2-3 hours. Important: decorate with powdered sugar AFTER thawing, never before.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Why doesn't Santiago Cake have flour?

Traditional Santiago Cake doesn't contain flour because it's an ancient recipe (documented since 1577) that uses only ground almonds as its base. This naturally makes it a gluten-free dessert, perfect for celiacs. The almonds provide the characteristic structure and texture of this Galician cake.

What does the cross on Santiago Cake mean?

The Cross of Santiago is the symbol of the Apostle Saint James and has become the hallmark of this cake. It represents the Jacobean tradition and the city of Santiago de Compostela. It's decorated by sprinkling powdered sugar over a template shaped like the cross, leaving its silhouette on the cake's surface. It's a way of honoring the pilgrimage route and Santiago's history.

Can it be made without cinnamon?

Yes, absolutely. Cinnamon is an optional ingredient in Santiago Cake. The traditional recipe protected by PGI allows it to be made with or without cinnamon. The essential flavors are almond, egg, and citrus. Some Galician families prefer it without cinnamon to better appreciate the pure almond flavor. It's a matter of personal preference.

Why did my cake turn out too dry?

The most common causes are: 1) Over-baking - bake only until the toothpick comes out clean, no more. 2) Poor quality or very dry almonds. 3) Not beating the eggs enough with the sugar in the initial step. 4) Lack of citrus zest, which provides moisture. Make sure to follow the exact times and use freshly bought almonds.

Can I use almonds with skin?

It's not recommended for the traditional recipe. Almonds should be blanched (white). The skin would impart a more bitter flavor and a less fine and elegant texture. Additionally, PGI regulations specify that it must be white almonds. If you use almonds with skin, you'll have a different cake, not the authentic Santiago Cake.

What is PGI and why is it important?

Protected Geographical Indication (PGI) is a European certification granted in 2006 that protects Santiago Cake. It guarantees that the cake is made in Galicia following the traditional recipe with specific ingredients: minimum 33% almonds, no flour, and proper proportion of eggs and sugar. It's a seal of quality and authenticity that preserves the centuries-old tradition.

💭 Final Reflection: More Than a Recipe

Every time we make Santiago Cake, we're not just making a dessert. We're connecting with centuries of Galician tradition, with the hands of the nuns who made it in the convents of Compostela, with the pilgrims who arrived exhausted in Santiago and found comfort in this sweet almond bite.

This cake is a symbol of hospitality, of Galician roots, of simplicity that becomes excellence. It's a reminder that sometimes the best results come from few quality ingredients and respecting traditional processes.
