Race for a Bed: The Crowded Camino de Santiago in the Age of Tour Packages

A candid look from the finish line. How the last stretch of the French Way has become a logistical maze between public hostels, private hostels, and organized tours.

By Susana & Antonio, writing from Quiroga Palacios, Santiago de Compostela
I’ve spent all of 2025 watching, from my window in Santiago, the final parade of pilgrims. I see them arrive: some with that peaceful glow of someone who’s lived something unique, others with faces marked by a stress they never expected on a spiritual journey. Their stories, shared over coffee or a beer, paint a picture very different from the idyllic postcards. Today, I want to share that picture—no filters—so that anyone setting out in 2026 does so with eyes wide open.

1. The End of Spontaneity (and the Rise of Big Business)

Let’s be honest: the romantic Camino of old—just a backpack, faith, and a handful of euros—clashes head-on with the reality of mass tourism. What used to be a low-cost adventure is now, especially from Sarria onwards, a high-yield tourist industry. Prices have shot up.

💰 Key Fact: Accommodation Budget

If you want to guarantee a bed by booking private hostels, you’ll need a budget of over €200 just for accommodation for the last stages. Public hostels (about €10) are the last holdout, but the real cost is the uncertainty and the long lines.

At times, you may feel less like a pilgrim and more like a walking wallet in an overcrowded circuit.

2. The New Reality: Tour Packages vs. Free Adventure

This is where the change really hits home. Companies and agencies have taken over what was once a unique, unpredictable adventure. Now, from Sarria or even earlier, you’re offered the “full package”: reserved accommodation, luggage transfer, detailed itinerary, and phone support.

For me, raised on the philosophy of “grab your backpack and just walk,” that option misses the point. It takes away the essence. But let’s be fair: in today’s context, if you don’t want to risk it, it’s not a bad choice. The mindset has changed. Maybe my way of seeing things, stuck in my 50s, is outdated. Maybe, for many today, true freedom isn’t about uncertainty, but about having everything sorted so you can just focus on walking.

It’s an open debate, but it’s the reality shaping the Camino now.

3. The Bunk Bed Lottery: Pure Stress

This is what happens if you go for the “free adventure” with no reservations. I’ve heard dozens of stories from groups who broke the harmony by waking up at 5 AM—not out of spirit, but out of fear of not finding a bed in Arzúa or O Pedrouzo. The image of pilgrims queuing for hours outside a public hostel, backpacks at their feet, has become normal.

Your day stops revolving around the scenery and starts revolving around the clock. Imagine you stop at a bar, connect with other pilgrims, and suddenly someone checks their phone and says, “It’s 11:45, I’m out.” The table empties in a flash. Want to stop to see that Romanesque chapel or sit in the shade of a pine? First you have to calculate: “If I stop now, will I make it to the hostel in Palas de Rei in time?”

And here’s the harshest comparison: the early hours can feel like ‘Squid Game’. No one dies, but it’s a daily race for a limited resource under invisible pressure. Solidarity is put to the test when, at dawn, you see other pilgrims not as companions, but as competitors for your place to sleep.

Now imagine the worst-case scenario: you twist your ankle. The pain is sharp, you need to stop. But what if you don’t have a bed? Logic flips. It’s no longer about seeking help, but about calculating if you can drag yourself the remaining kilometers to the hostel that gives you the right to collapse. It’s the height of absurdity.

4. Arriving in Santiago: From Epiphany to Endless Queues

The contrast can be brutal. After days of physical and emotional effort, reaching the dreamed-of finish line sometimes means more crowds: lines for the Compostela certificate, lines for restaurants, lines for ice cream, crowds in the Praza do Obradoiro. It’s a cold shower that can dampen the experience.

That’s why I say: it’s been years since I last walked the Camino, and honestly, I’d think twice about it today. Not because of the Camino itself, which is still wonderful, but because of everything around it.

5. The Winter Option: Less Romantic, Maybe More Real

There’s another way, less crowded and with its own challenges: walking the Camino between October and April. It’s less romantic weather-wise, but maybe more real if you want to escape the crowds.

❄️ The Harsh Side

Get ready for cold Galician winters and constant rain—they’re the norm. Many public hostels close in winter. Days are shorter and some routes, like the Napoleon variant in the Pyrenees, are off-limits.

✨ The Upside

In exchange, you’ll find empty trails, deep peace, and much lower prices at the private hostels that stay open. It’s an option for the tough ones, who’d rather battle cold and rain than crowds. It takes careful planning and solid gear, but it gives the Camino back some of its lost silence.

6. Emergency Plan & Public Hostel Map

Before wrapping up, here’s a practical section, written with the mix of seriousness and Galician humor the Camino now demands.

Conclusion: The Camino You Choose

The Camino de Santiago is still a deep, life-changing experience for many. But ignoring its new reality—crowds, commercialization, and logistical stress—does a disservice to anyone preparing to walk it.

The choice is more complex than ever: authentic adventure with the risk of burnout, peace of mind bought through a tour package, or the introspective challenge of winter? There’s no universal answer. Just honest info so everyone can choose their own path within the Camino.

I’m writing this because I love the Camino and want you to love it too—without stress ruining your experience. Come prepared, not scared.
